# Discovery Intake: Solo Tradie Business — Operating Model

## Context
This describes the operating model of a typical Australian solo trade business operator (1–2 person), working in residential trades such as plumbing, electrical, carpentry, painting, tiling, or general building. Based in Sydney. Annual revenue $80,000–$350,000. The tradie is technically skilled but has no formal business training. They use a smartphone daily, WhatsApp for client comms, and either basic Xero or a spreadsheet for invoicing. They work on the tools 6–9 hours a day and face 60–90 minutes of unpaid admin work every evening.

---

## Value Stream 1: Win Work (Lead Generation → Quote → Close)

The tradie gets work through three channels: word of mouth referrals from previous clients, direct enquiries via phone or email, and occasionally through platforms like Hipages or Airtasker. Referrals are the most reliable source — roughly 60% of jobs come from someone who knows someone. The tradie has no marketing strategy. Their "brand" is their reputation on the tools and whether previous clients remember to mention them.

When a lead comes in, it typically arrives as a phone call while the tradie is on site (they can't answer), a text message or WhatsApp message, or an email with a vague description of what the customer wants. The tradie responds when they get a chance — often 4–8 hours later, sometimes the next day. Some leads go cold because the response was too slow. The tradie doesn't track how many enquiries they miss or how long they take to respond.

For quoting, the tradie visits the site (unpaid), assesses the scope, estimates materials and labour time, and writes a quote. Most tradies quote from gut feel and experience rather than referencing their historical job data. They have no systematic way to know if they're underpricing or overpricing. A plumber might quote $3,800 for a bathroom renovation when their last four similar jobs averaged $4,600 — they simply don't have that data accessible. Quotes are typically sent as a text message, a PDF from an app like ServiceM8, or sometimes just a verbal number. There is no follow-up system. If the customer doesn't respond within a week, the tradie has usually moved on mentally. They don't track quote win rates by job type, location, or value.

Pain points in this value stream: leads going cold due to slow response times; no visibility of conversion rates; systematic under-quoting due to lack of pricing intelligence; no follow-up process for open quotes; unpaid site visits for quotes that don't convert; no way to identify which job types or areas are most profitable.

## Value Stream 2: Schedule & Plan Work

Once a job is won, the tradie needs to schedule it. Scheduling is done in the head, on a whiteboard at home, or in a phone calendar. There is no capacity planning. The tradie books jobs based on when the customer wants it done and what feels right. This leads to over-commitment (too many jobs in a week), under-commitment (gaps with no work), and poor geographic routing (driving across Sydney between jobs instead of clustering by area).

For each job, the tradie needs to confirm: the site is accessible, the customer will be home or has left a key, any council permits are in place, and materials are available. These confirmations happen informally — a text the night before, a phone call in the morning. If the customer doesn't confirm, the tradie sometimes drives to site only to find they can't access it.

Materials planning is reactive. The tradie knows roughly what they need but checks availability the morning of or buys on the way to site. For specialised items, they may need to order from Reece, Tradelink, or Middy's with lead times. If something is out of stock, the job stalls. There's no forward visibility of material requirements across upcoming jobs.

The tradie has no system for tracking which jobs are confirmed, which need materials ordered, and which have unresolved access issues. It's all in their head. This causes stress, double-bookings, forgotten confirmations, and wasted travel time.

Pain points: no capacity planning leads to feast-or-famine cycles; poor geographic routing wastes fuel and time; no automated confirmation process; materials ordered reactively causing delays; no forward view of the week's requirements; double-bookings and scheduling conflicts; no buffer for warranty callbacks or emergency work.

## Value Stream 3: Execute Work On Site

The tradie arrives at site, performs the work, and manages the job to completion. For simple jobs (tap replacement, power point install), this is straightforward — a few hours, done. For complex jobs (bathroom renovation, rewire), the work spans multiple days or weeks and involves coordination with other trades (tiler needs the plumber done first, electrician needs the plasterer done first).

Time tracking is non-existent or rough. The tradie doesn't log hours against jobs in any structured way. This means they have no accurate picture of labour cost per job, which feeds directly into the under-quoting problem. A job quoted at $2,500 that takes 3 days instead of 2 has effectively been done at a loss once materials and time are factored in, but the tradie doesn't know this because they don't track it.

Quality control is personal pride — the tradie does good work because that's who they are. There's no formal checklist, no photographic record of completed work (or if there are photos, they're scattered in the camera roll with personal photos), and no structured sign-off process. This matters when warranty claims arise months later and the tradie has no documentation of what was done.

When working with other trades on a multi-trade job, coordination is ad hoc. WhatsApp group messages, phone calls, and assumptions. If the plasterer is late, the painter can't start, and the timeline cascades. The tradie managing the job absorbs the client frustration even when the delay isn't their fault.

Compliance and safety obligations are significant. Electrical work requires licensing and must comply with AS/NZS 3000. Plumbing requires WaterMark certification. All trades need to manage workplace health and safety (WHS) obligations on site. Most solo tradies handle this through personal knowledge rather than documented processes.

Pain points: no time tracking means no visibility of actual job costs; no photographic documentation system; multi-trade coordination is entirely informal; no structured sign-off or completion process; compliance and safety documentation is ad hoc; scope creep on jobs goes untracked; no way to identify which job types consistently take longer than quoted.

## Value Stream 4: Get Paid (Invoicing → Collection → Reconciliation)

This is the value stream where most solo trade businesses fail. The tradie finishes a job on Friday, is exhausted, and the invoice doesn't go out until Monday — or Wednesday — or sometimes next week. Every day an invoice is delayed is a day cash flow is pushed out.

Invoicing is done in Xero, a spreadsheet, or sometimes handwritten. The tradie needs to remember the exact scope of work, materials used, and any variations from the original quote. If they haven't tracked this during the job, they're reconstructing from memory. This often means under-invoicing — forgetting a trip to the supplier, or not charging for an extra hour when the job ran over.

Once the invoice is sent, payment terms are typically 14 or 30 days. Residential customers often pay promptly, but some don't. Commercial jobs and property managers are the worst — 45–60 day payment cycles are common. The tradie has no automated follow-up system. If an invoice goes overdue, they might send a reminder text after a few weeks. Many tradies are uncomfortable chasing money. They'd rather write it off than have an awkward conversation with a client they might want future work from.

Cash flow management is reactive. The tradie checks their bank balance and either feels OK or panics. There's no forward projection of incoming payments vs upcoming expenses. BAS (Business Activity Statement) obligations arrive as quarterly shocks — the tradie suddenly owes the ATO $4,000–$12,000 and hasn't set the money aside. This is the single most common cause of financial distress in solo trade businesses.

Expense tracking is minimal. Fuel, materials, tool purchases, vehicle costs, insurance premiums — these are either in Xero if the tradie is diligent, or in a shoebox of receipts. At tax time (or BAS time), there's a panicked scramble to reconcile everything. The tradie's accountant charges more because the records are a mess.

Pain points: delayed invoicing directly causes cash flow problems; invoices often under-capture the actual work done; no automated payment follow-up; overdue invoices go unchased for weeks; no forward cash flow visibility; BAS arrives as a financial shock; expense tracking is ad hoc; bank reconciliation is manual or non-existent; the tradie doesn't know their actual profit margin on any given job.

## Value Stream 5: Manage the Business (Admin, Compliance, Insurance, Tax)

Beyond the daily operations, the tradie must manage ongoing business obligations. This is the work they hate most because it's invisible — it doesn't produce income and it feels like bureaucracy.

Licensing and registration: trade licences must be maintained and renewed. The tradie needs to track CPD (Continuing Professional Development) points for some licences. ABN registration, GST registration, and WorkCover insurance must be kept current. Missing a renewal can mean they legally can't work.

Insurance: public liability insurance is essential ($10–20M cover is standard). Professional indemnity may be needed for certain trades. Tool and vehicle insurance. Income protection. Most tradies have these but don't review them annually. They're often underinsured or paying for coverage they don't need.

Tax and BAS: quarterly BAS lodgement, annual tax return, superannuation contributions (which sole traders often skip), and record keeping. The tradie relies on an accountant but provides poor records, leading to higher accounting fees and potentially missed deductions.

Vehicle and tool management: the ute or van needs servicing, registration, and insurance. Tools need replacement and calibration. There's no asset register — the tradie doesn't know the replacement value of their tool kit, which can easily be $15,000–$50,000.

Business development and reputation: the tradie does no active marketing. They rely entirely on word of mouth. They may have a Google Business listing that they set up once and never updated. They don't ask for Google reviews despite this being the most powerful free marketing channel available. They don't have a website, or have one that was built five years ago and looks terrible on mobile.

Pain points: licence and insurance renewals are tracked mentally and sometimes missed; no visibility of upcoming compliance deadlines; poor record keeping increases accounting costs; BAS and tax obligations are reactive not planned; no asset register for tools and equipment; no active reputation management; missed marketing opportunities through Google reviews and referral programs; no business planning or financial forecasting; the tradie has no idea what their business is actually worth.

## Value Stream 6: Build and Maintain Client Relationships

The tradie's most valuable business asset is their client relationships, but they manage them like personal friendships rather than business assets. There's no CRM. Client history lives in the tradie's head, in scattered text messages, and in whatever invoicing records exist.

When a previous client calls, the tradie may or may not remember the details of past work. "Was it the Henderson bathroom I did two years ago, or was that the Wilsons?" They can't quickly look up what was done, what was charged, or whether there were any issues.

Warranty and callback management is reactive. If a customer calls with a problem, the tradie responds based on their memory of the job. There's no ticketing system, no documentation of warranty claims, and no data on whether certain job types or materials generate more callbacks than others.

Repeat business and referral generation is entirely organic. The tradie doesn't proactively reach out to past clients. They don't send a message six months after a bathroom renovation saying "How's everything holding up?" They don't ask for referrals or reviews. They wait for the phone to ring.

Client communication is inconsistent. Some tradies are responsive and communicative. Others go silent for days during a job, leaving the customer anxious about progress. There's no system for sending progress updates, completion notifications, or post-job follow-ups.

Pain points: no client database or CRM of any kind; job history is inaccessible; no proactive client relationship management; warranty claims handled ad hoc; no systematic referral or review generation; inconsistent communication damages trust; high-value repeat clients aren't identified or nurtured; no segmentation of clients by value or behaviour.

## Roles

- **The Tradie** — performs all operational work on the tools, manages all client relationships, does all quoting and invoicing, handles all scheduling, makes all business decisions. This single-point dependency on one person is the fundamental structural constraint of the entire operating model.
- **The Customer** — residential homeowner or property manager who needs trade work. Ranges from one-off clients to long-term repeat customers.
- **The Accountant** — external, typically seen quarterly or annually. Relies on whatever records the tradie provides.
- **Supplier** — Reece, Tradelink, Middy's, Bunnings. Provides materials. Relationship is transactional.
- **Other Trades** — subcontractors or complementary tradies (plasterer, tiler, electrician) needed on multi-trade jobs. Coordination is informal.

## Key Metrics (Mostly Untracked)

- Quote win rate (unknown — typically 30–50%)
- Average response time to leads (unknown — typically 4–24 hours)
- Invoice-to-payment cycle (unknown — typically 14–45 days)
- Actual job profitability vs quoted (unknown — frequently negative on complex jobs)
- Overdue invoice rate (unknown — typically 15–25% of invoices go past terms)
- Customer lifetime value (unknown — repeat customers are 3–5x more valuable)
- Weekly billable vs non-billable hours (unknown — admin typically 8–12 hours/week unpaid)

## Controls and Constraints

- Trade licensing (state-issued, must be current to legally operate)
- GST registration and quarterly BAS obligations
- WorkCover / workplace insurance requirements
- Australian Standards compliance (AS/NZS 3000 for electrical, AS/NZS 3500 for plumbing)
- Council permits for certain work categories
- Privacy obligations for client data (informal but real)
- Working hours constraints (physical fatigue limits effective work to 8–9 hours, admin on top)
- Single-person capacity constraint (everything depends on one human being)
